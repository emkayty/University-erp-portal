## 1. How to use this package

This package has two parts. The first is the **12-slide decision-maker deck**. It is designed for an executive briefing, institutional discovery session, policy discussion, or controlled pilot approval meeting. It should be presented as a strategic operating-model conversation, not as a technical tour of every screen.

The second is this **companion manual**. It explains what each slide means, how to demonstrate the product, what data is illustrative, how each audience can use the system, which decisions belong to the institution, and what evidence is required before production use.

### 1.1 Recommended meeting formats

| Meeting format             | Suggested duration | Primary outcome                                                                         |
| -------------------------- | -----------------: | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Executive briefing         |      15–20 minutes | Shared understanding of the problem, value, and decision required.                      |
| Institutional discovery    |      45–60 minutes | Selection of the first high-value process and named owners.                             |
| Senate/Registry workshop   |      60–90 minutes | Review of academic lifecycle, policy, result governance, and evidence.                  |
| ICT/security review        |     60–120 minutes | Review of architecture, access, privacy, integration, recovery, and UAT gates.          |
| Pilot approval meeting     |      30–45 minutes | Approval of scope, responsibilities, acceptance measures, and decision gates.           |
| Partner or funder briefing |      20–30 minutes | Explanation of institutional value, differentiators, delivery model, and risk controls. |

### 1.2 What the deck is and is not

The deck is an executive product and governance briefing. It is not a live production report, a promise of regulatory certification, a replacement for the technical manual, or a substitute for the institution’s policies. The numbers and names in the mock screens are fictional and illustrative. They exist to make the operating model visible.

### 1.3 The recommended presentation posture

Present UniPortal as a **platform for institutional accountability**. Avoid presenting it as an autonomous decision-maker or as a magical replacement for professional university offices. The strongest position is that UniPortal gives authorized people a clearer, more connected, more evidence-led way to perform the decisions they already own.

## 2. The decision-maker story

### 2.1 The problem

A university can have competent people and still experience institutional friction when admissions, academic records, assessment, fees, services, and governance operate through disconnected handoffs. The result is repeated data entry, unclear responsibility, delayed decisions, inconsistent records, weak exception visibility, and difficulty proving how a high-impact decision was made.

### 2.2 The product answer

UniPortal connects the university’s student lifecycle and surrounding institutional operations in one role-aware environment. It gives applicants and students a clearer path, gives staff focused workspaces, gives academic leaders stronger control of academic processes, gives Finance and HR governed operational workflows, and gives executives access to current operational signals.

### 2.3 The governance answer

The platform is designed around authority, scope, lifecycle, evidence, and audit. The user interface changes based on effective role and scope, while API authorization and database protections remain the enforcement boundary. High-impact processes such as admissions, results, payments, privacy, payroll, and identity cards require controlled states, reasons, or review evidence.

### 2.4 The institutional decision

The decision is not simply whether the university likes the interface. The decision is whether the institution is willing to select one high-value process, name its owners, validate its data and policy, test the system with real roles, certify required providers, and measure the results before expanding.

> The recommended decision is: **approve a focused institutional discovery and controlled pilot, subject to explicit evidence gates.**

## 4. Slide-by-slide presenter manual

### Cover — UniPortal ERP

**Purpose.** Establish the product as a university operating platform and set an executive, institutional tone.

**Say this.** “Today’s briefing is about how a university can move from fragmented administration to one connected operating picture—from applicant to alumni, and across the academic, financial, people, service, and governance work that supports that journey.”

**Emphasize.** The subtitle is deliberately broader than “student portal.” UniPortal is intended to connect the student-facing journey with the internal work that makes reliable outcomes possible.

**Do not imply.** The cover does not claim unrestricted production certification. The small release-status label is intentional.

**Transition.** “To understand the value, we should first name the operating problem.”

### Slide 1 — The university needs one accountable operating picture

**Purpose.** Make fragmentation a leadership problem rather than an ICT-only problem.

**Say this.** “Most universities do not lack data. They lack one accountable view of how data, decisions, owners, and next actions connect. A student can be accepted in one process, registered in another, charged in a third, assessed in a fourth, and cleared through a manual path that no single office can see completely.”

**Explain the visual.** Admissions, academics, finance, services, and governance all generate important information. When they converge only through manual reconciliation, the university becomes dependent on memory, email, spreadsheets, and late reports.

**Ask the room.** “Which journey currently costs the institution the most time, trust, or revenue?”

**Transition.** “UniPortal responds by connecting the lifecycle rather than treating each office as a separate island.”

### Slide 2 — UniPortal connects the journey from applicant to alumni

**Purpose.** Establish the product’s scope and its lifecycle-first design.

**Say this.** “UniPortal begins before admission and continues beyond graduation. It connects application, verification, offer, clearance, matriculation, registration, learning, assessment, examination, results, progression, graduation, and alumni continuity. Around that journey sit finance, people operations, campus services, research, policies, privacy, audit, reports, and reliability.”

**Explain the value.** The same student journey generates consequences across multiple offices. A registration decision affects learning and assessment. A result affects progression and graduation. A fee-clearance policy affects access to services and graduation readiness. Connecting the lifecycle makes these relationships visible.

**Decision implication.** A university can begin with one journey but should design the pilot with its upstream and downstream dependencies visible.

**Transition.** “Connected does not mean everyone sees everything. The next principle is operating context.”

### Slide 3 — Every user receives the right operating context

**Purpose.** Explain role, scope, and least-privilege behavior in language that non-technical leaders can understand.

**Say this.** “The student needs a personal academic journey. The lecturer needs assigned offerings and gradebooks. Registry needs academic lifecycle authority. Leadership and ICT need oversight, policy, security, and reliability. These are not the same job, so they should not have the same workspace.”

**Explain effective context.** UniPortal distinguishes stored role from currently effective role and scope. A time-bounded delegation or assignment can change what the user is allowed to operate without rewriting historical identity. Department/faculty boundaries and ownership can further narrow the view.

**Policy point.** A hidden menu is not the complete security model. Backend authorization and database protections remain responsible for enforcement.

**Transition.** “Let us now look at what an executive sees when the institution is operating through this model.”

### Slide 4 — Executive command centre

**Purpose.** Demonstrate how the executive dashboard translates institutional information into attention and action.

**Say this.** “This is not presented as a live university report. It is an illustrative command-centre demonstration. The point is to show the type of operating picture leadership can use: active students, applications in review, financial clearance, results readiness, attention queues, flow, operational readiness, and audit/governance context.”

**Point to the mock.**

1. The **Attention Queue** turns invisible operational pressure into named work.
2. The **Application-to-Matriculation Flow** helps leadership see movement and potential drop-off.
3. The **Operational Readiness** card provides a summary that can lead to investigation.
4. The **Audit & Governance** card connects executive attention with controls and open issues.

**Data rule.** State explicitly that 6,240 active students, 1,842 applications in review, 87% financial clearance, 94% results ready, and other figures are fictitious demonstration data.

**Executive question.** “Which three indicators would your leadership team need to see every morning, and which office would own each response?”

**Transition.** “The executive view is useful only if it improves the experience and responsibility of the people inside the journey.”

### Slide 5 — Student journey

**Purpose.** Demonstrate the product’s student-centred experience and mobile-first presentation.

**Say this.** “A student should not have to interpret ten unrelated menus to understand what is happening. The Academic Life view puts readiness, next legitimate actions, degree progress, current courses, CGPA, and history into one context.”

**Point to the mock.**

- Amina Yusuf is a fictional student in B.Sc. Computer Science.
- The readiness ring is a demonstration of how the system can summarize current state; it is not a clinical or academic verdict.
- The next-action list shows tasks that the student can legitimately perform or resolve.
- The desktop and phone views show the same priorities in a responsive layout.

**Value explanation.** This can reduce avoidable enquiries, help students act earlier, and give advisers a clearer starting point. The institution must still decide what constitutes readiness, what information is shown, and which office owns each intervention.

**Transition.** “The next mock demonstrates how UniPortal supports the academic staff member who carries a large operational burden.”

### Slide 6 — Lecturer workspace and large-cohort grading

**Purpose.** Show that the product is designed for real teaching and assessment scale.

**Say this.** “A lecturer managing 347 students cannot rely on an unstructured spreadsheet and hope that every mark is correct. The assessment workspace combines an authorized offering, a paginated gradebook, validated CSV upload, visible persistence status, audit context, and Results Assurance signals.”

**Point to the mock.**

- **347 enrolled** and **332 marks saved** demonstrate cohort scale.
- **15 need review** demonstrates that incomplete work should be visible rather than silently accepted.
- **95.7% completeness** and **3 review signals** are illustrative review indicators.
- **No out-of-range marks** means no such signal was present in the demonstration data; it is not proof that a real class is correct.
- The grade table is paginated so the user does not need to load every student at once.

**Policy point.** Results Assurance supports academic review. It does not determine misconduct, fail a student automatically, or replace moderation and approval.

**Transition.** “Assessment is one part of the academic record. Registry also needs a controlled view of active students and institutional identity.”

### Slide 7 — Registry operations, identity, and active-student records

**Purpose.** Demonstrate the intersection of records work, privacy, configurable identifiers, and physical identity-card operations.

**Say this.** “Registry needs operational visibility of active students, but ‘full information’ must mean the information necessary for a legitimate task—not unrestricted exposure of NIN, medical data, or every sensitive identifier. The directory therefore uses scope, search, filters, pagination, and a privacy-aware projection.”

**Point to the mock.**

1. The active-student directory is records-scoped and shows the current result window, not an unbounded export.
2. The institution can configure matriculation formats using approved tokens such as institution, faculty, department, programme, year, entry year, and padded sequence.
3. The identity-card register supports selection of active cards and a controlled A4 PDF workflow.
4. The preview represents one A4 page with five vertical rows; each row places an ATM/ID-1-size card front beside the matching back horizontally.

**Policy point.** Identifier changes apply to future issuance and should not silently renumber historical students. Artwork, card fields, expiry, and physical printing must be institutionally approved.

**Transition.** “The common thread across these screens is governance.”

### Slide 8 — Governance is built into the workflow

**Purpose.** Move the audience from interface admiration to institutional control.

**Say this.** “A high-impact action should not be only a button. It should have an authority, an allowed state transition, an evidence path, a reason where required, and a traceable history.”

**Explain the left/right contrast.** Without governance, approvals remain in messages, evidence is missing, and accountability is difficult to prove. With UniPortal, admissions decisions, result finalization, fee waivers, payment reconciliation, clearance, privacy requests, security incidents, and identity-card actions can follow controlled paths.

**Institutional ownership.** UniPortal supports human judgement. It does not replace Senate, Registry, Finance, HR, the DPO, security leadership, or approved university policy.

**Transition.** “These controls are valuable because they improve outcomes across several stakeholder groups at once.”

### Slide 9 — One platform, multiple institutional outcomes

**Purpose.** Give each decision-maker a concise value case.

**Say this.** “The product’s value is not one feature. It is the combination of better student experience, stronger academic confidence, financial control, institutional intelligence, workforce efficiency, and privacy/trust.”

**Facilitation technique.** Invite each audience group to choose one card:

- Students and student affairs: student experience.
- Senate, Registry, Deans, and HODs: academic confidence.
- Bursary: financial control.
- VC and leadership: institutional intelligence.
- ICT and operations: workforce efficiency and reliability.
- DPO/security and Council: privacy and trust.

**Guardrail.** Do not claim invented percentage savings or adoption levels. Agree measures for a pilot and report actual baseline-to-after evidence.

**Transition.** “The next question is why UniPortal rather than another collection of modules.”

### Slide 10 — What makes UniPortal different

**Purpose.** Provide defensible product differentiation.

**Say this.** “UniPortal is differentiated by how the pieces work together: lifecycle-first design, role and scope context, evidence-led intelligence, configurable institutional identity, and operational discipline.”

**Explain the layered visual.** Policy and governance sit above role, scope, ownership, and lifecycle. Connected university operations sit at the centre. Evidence, audit, responsible intelligence, integration, and recovery support the operating model below.

**Competitive language.** Use “designed around,” “supports,” and “implemented in the current source line” where appropriate. Avoid saying “fully autonomous,” “zero-risk,” or “automatically compliant.”

**Transition.** “Differentiation matters only when the institution can adopt the product safely.”

### Slide 11 — A responsible path from demonstration to institutional value

**Purpose.** Turn interest into a safe, measurable next step.

**Say this.** “The responsible next step is not to activate every module at once. It is to select one high-value journey, name the people who own it, validate the data and policy, test real roles and failure paths, certify external providers, and expand only after evidence is signed.”

**Explain the sequence.**

1. **Institutional discovery:** identify the journey, process owner, policy owner, data owner, and technical owner.
2. **Controlled pilot:** configure, test, train, observe, and resolve exceptions.
3. **Evidence-based expansion:** accept only after UAT, provider, security, backup/restore, recovery, performance, and governance evidence.

**Status point.** The current release is controlled staging / pre-production ready. It should not be sold as unrestricted production-certified before the remaining gates are complete.

**Transition.** “That leads to the decision we are asking the institution to make today.”

### Slide 12 — The decision

**Purpose.** Secure a clear, responsible commitment.

**Say this.** “The decision is whether to approve a focused institutional discovery and controlled pilot. We are not asking the institution to waive governance. We are asking it to select a high-value journey and create the evidence required for a confident decision about expansion.”

**Ask the four questions.**

1. Which journey becomes the first measurable pilot?
2. Who owns the business decision and the data-quality decision?
3. Which policy and provider dependencies need certification first?
4. What evidence will the institution require before expansion?

**Close.** “UniPortal helps the university move from fragmented administration to coordinated, accountable execution.”

**After the meeting.** Record the answers, owners, pilot population, data sources, policies, dependencies, acceptance measures, and decision date. Do not treat general enthusiasm as an implementation approval.

## 5. Mock demonstration data and accuracy rules

### 5.1 What “real data” means in the deck

The requested mock demonstration uses **realistic operational patterns and plausible values**, not real personal or institutional records. This is the correct approach for a decision-maker presentation because it makes the product understandable without exposing confidential student, staff, payment, or academic information.

The following values are explicitly illustrative:

| Mock area                | Demonstration values                                                                                                                                                               |
| ------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Executive command centre | 6,240 active students; 1,842 applications in review; 87% financial clearance; 94% results ready; 15 gradebooks needing completion; 3 data-quality reviews; 11 fee-waiver requests. |
| Student journey          | Amina Yusuf; B.Sc. Computer Science; 82% journey readiness; 92 of 120 credits; 6 current courses; CGPA 3.72.                                                                       |
| Assessment               | CSC 305 Data Structures; 2025/2026; Section A; 347 enrolled; 332 marks saved; 15 need review; 95.7% completeness; 3 review signals; no out-of-range marks.                         |
| Registry and identity    | Fictional student names, identifiers, programme, level, active-card status, records-scope directory, 2,146 illustrative active-directory total, five-row horizontal front/back ID-1 card preview.       |

### 5.2 Accuracy rules for a live demo

Before demonstrating with live institutional data, the presenter must:

1. Use a non-production or explicitly approved demonstration tenant.
2. Use fictional or masked student, staff, payment, and academic records.
3. Label the screen or slide as “Illustrative demonstration data” where feasible.
4. Avoid showing NIN, BVN, medical information, private contact information, passwords, MFA codes, payment tokens, or real provider credentials.
5. Use accounts representing real roles and scopes rather than using a super-admin account for everything.
6. Ensure the presenter understands which numbers are calculated, which are configured, and which are static mockups.
7. Never create fake academic records in a production database merely to make a screen look populated.
8. Do not claim that a mock metric is a live institutional result.

### 5.3 What is accurate about the visual design

The mockups use the implemented UniPortal design direction: deep blue primary colour, restrained gold accent, pale-blue surfaces, grouped workspace navigation, role chips, attention cards, responsive presentation, Results Assurance, active-student search, configurable identity-card policy, and a five-row A4 card-printing concept with horizontal front/back pairs. The screens are visual demonstrations, not screenshots of a live database or a promise that every displayed sample metric already exists in the current environment.

### 5.4 What must be verified in the actual software

A live UAT session must verify the real API response shapes, role gates, empty states, pagination, provider status, data quality, calculation rules, physical card alignment, and the exact institution’s configured policies. Generated mock screens are not a substitute for that verification.

## 6. Live demonstration operating procedure

### 6.1 Before the meeting

The presenter should confirm the environment, login accounts, browser, network, database state, Redis/worker health, prepared demonstration data, and current release. The API live and readiness checks, worker health check, and frontend health should be tested before the audience arrives. If the environment is not ready, use the approved visual mockups and state that the demonstration is illustrative.

The presenter should have a written fallback script. A failed live demo should become an honest explanation of operational readiness, not a reason to improvise or expose sensitive debugging information.

### 6.2 During the meeting

Begin with the problem, not the menu. Use one coherent storyline. Show a student journey, then the lecturer’s assessment context, then Registry’s controlled records/identity workflow, then executive governance. Explain every number as illustrative or live. Pause after each major workflow to ask what the audience would need to approve it institutionally.

### 6.3 After the meeting

Record questions by category: process, policy, data, security/privacy, integration, user experience, performance, cost, implementation, and governance. Assign an owner and answer date. Keep a decision log. Do not commit to a feature, integration, timeline, or certification claim without confirming the source, owner, and evidence.

### 6.4 Failure-handling script

If a screen fails, say:

> “The underlying release is still subject to controlled testing. I will not claim that a failed demo is acceptable, and I will not bypass the control. We will record the exact failure, identify whether it is environment, data, permission, provider, or application behavior, and return with evidence.”

This response demonstrates the governance culture the product is designed to support.

## 7. How each institutional audience should use UniPortal

### 7.1 Vice-Chancellor and executive leadership

Leadership should use the executive dashboard, institutional analytics, reports, attention queues, readiness summaries, policy views, audit/governance information, and reliability context to ask better questions. Leadership should not use the system to bypass Registry, Senate, Finance, HR, DPO, or security authority.

**Typical use:** Review institutional signals, assign attention, approve policy direction, monitor strategic initiatives, and request evidence from the responsible office.

### 7.2 Governing Council and policy-makers

Council and policy-makers should use the platform to understand the proposed operating model, control environment, data responsibilities, policy dependencies, risk posture, and pilot evidence. They should ask whether decisions are accountable, whether sensitive records are protected, and whether the institution can recover from failure.

**Typical use:** Approve governance principles, risk appetite, retention rules, production gates, and institutional accountability.

### 7.3 Senate and academic leadership

Senate, Deans, and HODs should use academic structures, curriculum, offerings, assessments, results assurance, examination, progression, appeals, graduation, and academic analytics. They should confirm that the software reflects approved academic policy and does not silently convert a policy decision into a technical default.

**Typical use:** Review academic readiness, course/result status, exceptions, assurance signals, and policy-controlled approval paths.

### 7.4 Registrar and Registry

Registry should use admissions, applications, verification, student records, curriculum, course offerings, identity cards, examinations, results governance, clearance, graduation, active-student directory, and audit. Registry should own academic record standards, lifecycle statuses, identifier policy, document/evidence policy, and records correction governance.

**Typical use:** Process lifecycle events, review evidence, correct authorized records, issue identifiers/cards, and maintain academic truth.

### 7.5 Bursar and Finance

The Bursary should use fee schedules, invoices, payment paths, reconciliation, waivers, clearance, revenue views, and financial reports. Finance should define provider arrangements, approval authority, reconciliation tolerance, manual fallback, and financial retention.

**Typical use:** Configure approved charges, reconcile provider events, review exceptions, approve waivers, and publish clearance signals according to policy.

### 7.6 HR and payroll

HR should use staff records, salary grades, leave, employment status, staff identity cards, payroll runs, payslips, and approved export paths. HR should own employment data quality, access removal triggers, leave policy, and payroll approval separation.

**Typical use:** Maintain staff truth, approve people operations, review payroll, and control access lifecycle.

### 7.7 ICT and platform operations

ICT should use user administration, role delegation, settings, feature flags, reliability, health checks, audit, integration configuration, logs, deployment guides, and backup/recovery runbooks. ICT should not decide academic or financial policy without the relevant owner.

**Typical use:** Keep the platform available, secure, observable, recoverable, and correctly configured.

### 7.8 DPO, privacy, and security

DPO and security staff should use privacy operations, subject verification, exports, rectification, erasure/pseudonymization, restriction, legal hold, incident management, audit, and security scope controls.

**Typical use:** Handle privacy and security matters using documented authority, reason, evidence, and retention rules.

### 7.9 Lecturers and operational staff

Lecturers should use assigned course offerings, gradebooks, CSV upload, Results Assurance, learning tools, attendance or evidence paths, announcements, and student progress. Operational staff should use only their assigned scopes—such as admissions, records, finance clerk, library, hostel, health, research, or transport.

**Typical use:** Complete daily work within the correct scope and escalate exceptions through the responsible authority.

### 7.10 Students and applicants

Students and applicants should use the public application, tracking, Academic Life, registration, results, fees, clearance, learning, services, identity-card, notifications, and profile functions appropriate to their lifecycle state.

**Typical use:** Provide accurate information, complete the next legitimate action, review status, and raise a governed support request when blocked.

## 8. What UniPortal does and why it matters

### 8.1 Admissions

UniPortal guides application capture, programme choices, personal information, examination evidence, previous education, O’Level results, supporting documents, consent, fees, review, submission, tracking, correction, verification, screening, offers, waitlists, deferment, and acceptance.

**Why it matters:** Admissions is the first institutional trust moment. Accurate capture and traceable decisions reduce avoidable disputes and improve the transition from applicant to student.

### 8.2 Student records and academic lifecycle

The platform supports students, programmes, departments, faculties, curriculum versions, course registration, academic plans, degree audits, academic history, appeals, transfers, interruptions, progression, clearance, graduation, and alumni continuity.

**Why it matters:** A student record is not a static profile. It is the authoritative history of a person’s institutional relationship.

### 8.3 Assessment, examinations, and results

The platform supports assessment schemes, gradebooks, validated bulk upload, live mark updates, assurance signals, examination context, result finalization, approval/publication states, transcript-related workflows, and controlled amendments.

**Why it matters:** Results affect progression, employment, professional identity, and institutional credibility. The workflow must support speed without sacrificing evidence.

### 8.4 Finance and clearance

The platform supports fee schedules, invoices, payment paths, reconciliation, waivers, clearance, and revenue reporting.

**Why it matters:** Students need clarity about what they owe and why. Finance needs evidence of what was charged, paid, reversed, waived, reconciled, or held for review.

### 8.5 People and payroll

HR and payroll workflows support staff records, salary grades, leave, payroll runs, payslips, and approved export paths.

**Why it matters:** Workforce operations require privacy, separation of duties, accurate employment status, and reliable execution.

### 8.6 Campus services

Library, hostel, clinic, transport, calendar, and identity-card workflows connect service delivery to the institutional lifecycle.

**Why it matters:** These services shape student welfare and operational trust. A student should not become a different person in every service office.

### 8.7 Governance and intelligence

Reports, analytics, data quality, academic journey readiness, Results Assurance, policies, privacy operations, security incidents, audit, reliability, user administration, and settings help the institution see and govern the system.

**Why it matters:** A university cannot improve what it cannot see, and it cannot safely automate what it cannot govern.

## 9. When and where UniPortal should be used

### 9.1 When to use it

UniPortal should be used whenever an authorized university process creates, changes, approves, publishes, reconciles, reviews, or retains institutional information. Examples include an admission decision, student status change, course registration, mark entry, result approval, fee waiver, payment reconciliation, leave decision, staff deactivation, privacy request, incident, identity-card issue, or policy update.

### 9.2 When not to bypass it

Users should not use private spreadsheets, informal messages, or unlogged manual processes to bypass an available governed workflow. If the workflow is missing, blocked, or inappropriate, the user should report the gap to the responsible process owner and record the approved temporary procedure.

### 9.3 Where to use it

The current web experience is designed for desktop, tablet, and mobile-responsive use. Students may use it on common personal devices, while staff may use institutional workstations or approved devices. Sensitive operations should be performed only on trusted devices and networks according to institutional policy.

### 9.4 Where data belongs

Authoritative institutional records belong in the governed system and its approved private storage/integration boundaries. Public chat messages, unsecured personal drives, unmanaged removable media, and unapproved third-party applications should not become shadow records for official university decisions.

### 9.5 Where the platform fits in the technology landscape

UniPortal consists of a web frontend, an API, PostgreSQL, Redis, BullMQ workers, shared contracts/utilities, and private file storage. External providers such as payment, SMS, email, S3, JAMB, WAEC/O’Level, and LTI connect through controlled integration paths. The university must decide which system is authoritative for each data domain and how synchronization is reconciled.

## 10. Decision, policy, and governance manual

### 10.1 Decisions the institution must own

The institution must own admission rules, academic progression, grading, absence semantics, result publication, result amendment, clearance, fees, waivers, retention, legal hold, privacy, identity numbering, card design, role separation, delegation, provider activation, and production approval.

### 10.2 Decisions the software can support

UniPortal can support configurable formats, feature flags, policy values, workflows, approval paths, roles, scopes, audit, evidence, reports, alerts, and operational signals. Configuration should not be mistaken for policy approval.

### 10.3 Role governance

The university should appoint role owners and review access periodically. High-risk roles such as Super Admin, VC, Bursar, and other privileged operators should use MFA where required and have controlled delegation. Temporary assignments should have start/end dates and a documented reason.

### 10.4 Data governance

For every major record type, define the authoritative owner, permitted users, correction process, retention period, legal-hold behavior, export approval, and deletion/pseudonymization approach. “All active-student information” should be interpreted through legitimate purpose and minimum necessary access.

### 10.5 Academic governance

The platform should reflect approved Senate and academic-board decisions. When a policy changes, the institution should record its effective date, affected academic period, retroactive/non-retroactive behavior, exception handling, and communication plan.

### 10.6 Financial governance

Finance should approve fee schedules, payment providers, reconciliation rules, waiver authority, refund paths, clearance semantics, and manual-fallback procedures. Provider credentials and webhook behavior require certification before live use.

### 10.7 Privacy and security governance

Privacy and security governance should define lawful purpose, subject verification, access review, incident escalation, breach communication, retention, legal hold, export handling, and staff responsibilities. The system supports these processes but does not create institutional policy automatically.

### 10.8 Production governance

Production release should require sign-off from business owners, ICT, security/privacy, data owners, provider owners, and leadership. A successful build is necessary but not sufficient for production approval.

## 11. Controlled pilot operating model

### 11.1 Select the pilot

Choose a workflow that is important, bounded, measurable, and owned. Suitable candidates include admissions and student records, course offerings and assessment, results governance, fees and clearance, or the student self-service journey.

### 11.2 Create the pilot charter

The charter should specify:

| Charter item     | Required description                                               |
| ---------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Pilot objective  | The institutional problem to solve.                                |
| Scope            | The academic period, population, offices, and workflow boundaries. |
| Out of scope     | Processes intentionally excluded from the first pilot.             |
| Business owner   | The office accountable for the outcome.                            |
| Data owner       | The person accountable for data quality and meaning.               |
| Technical owner  | The ICT person accountable for environment and integration.        |
| Policy owner     | The authority accountable for rules and exceptions.                |
| User group       | Real roles and representative users.                               |
| Success measures | Baseline, target, evidence source, and review date.                |
| Risk controls    | Privacy, security, rollback, support, and manual fallback.         |
| Exit decision    | The criteria for stop, remediate, or expand.                       |

### 11.3 Prepare data

Profile source data, resolve duplicates, classify missing values, confirm identifiers, map academic structures, validate fee references, and approve migration rules. Never use raw SQL to fabricate real student academic records for a demonstration or pilot.

### 11.4 Configure roles and policy

Set institution settings, roles, scopes, delegations, feature flags, academic policies, financial policies, identifier formats, card templates, calendars, and provider configuration. Record each decision and its owner.

### 11.5 Test the happy path and exception path

For admissions, test incomplete evidence, correction requests, provider unavailability, duplicate attempts, and decision reversal. For assessment, test missing marks, out-of-range marks, duplicate CSV rows, unauthorized offerings, large cohorts, and amendment history. For Finance, test pending, failed, reversed, duplicate, and manually reconciled payments. For privacy, test verification, export, correction, restriction, legal hold, and pseudonymization.

### 11.6 Run UAT with real roles

The student, lecturer, HOD, Dean, Registry, Bursary, HR, ICT, DPO/security, and leadership perspectives should be tested separately. A super-admin-only test cannot validate role isolation or user comprehension.

### 11.7 Decide expansion

The expansion decision should consider functional success, data quality, user adoption, support load, security evidence, provider certification, backup/restore, recovery, performance, audit, policy approval, and institutional confidence.

## 12. Questions decision-makers should ask

### Product and value

1. Which current workflow will improve first?
2. How will the institution measure the improvement?
3. Which offices need to collaborate for the value to appear?
4. What is the student-facing benefit?
5. What is the leadership-facing benefit?

### Data and records

1. Which system is authoritative for each record type?
2. How are duplicates and corrections handled?
3. What data is sensitive, and who may see it?
4. How are historical records retained when policy changes?
5. How do we prevent an attractive dashboard from hiding poor data quality?

### Academic governance

1. Who owns grading, progression, result publication, and amendments?
2. Which policy values are configurable and which require formal approval?
3. How are exceptions, appeals, and interruptions recorded?
4. How does the system preserve result provenance?
5. How are students and staff informed of changes?

### Security and privacy

1. How are high-risk roles protected with MFA and access review?
2. How are department/faculty boundaries enforced?
3. What prevents a frontend filter from becoming a false security boundary?
4. How are privacy requests, incidents, exports, and legal holds handled?
5. What evidence will we require from live RLS, backup, recovery, and penetration testing?

### Operations and resilience

1. What happens when a payment, SMS, email, or verification provider is unavailable?
2. What happens when a background job fails?
3. How are retries, dead letters, and manual review handled?
4. How do we monitor database, Redis, API, worker, and frontend health?
5. How quickly can we restore the system and verify data integrity?

### Adoption and sustainability

1. Who trains users and who supports them?
2. How will policy changes be communicated?
3. What is the cost of ongoing data quality and governance?
4. How will the institution avoid creating shadow spreadsheets again?
5. What is the expansion decision after the first pilot?

## 13. Acceptance measures and evidence

The institution should establish baseline measures before the pilot. The following are examples, not guaranteed product results.

### Student and applicant measures

Measure application completion, status-enquiry resolution, registration completion, support volume, time to find academic information, student understanding of next action, and mobile usability.

### Academic measures

Measure course-offering setup time, lecturer-assignment accuracy, time to enter marks, CSV rejection and correction rate, incomplete-gradebook count, result-review turnaround, assurance-signal resolution, and amendment traceability.

### Registry measures

Measure application processing time, unresolved verification cases, duplicate records, time to matriculation, correction turnaround, active-student search time, identity-card production reconciliation, and graduation-clearance turnaround.

### Finance measures

Measure invoice turnaround, payment reconciliation delay, unresolved provider callbacks, waiver review time, clearance exceptions, and report-preparation time.

### Control measures

Measure inappropriate-access attempts, access-review completion, audit search time, privacy-request turnaround, incident-response evidence, backup/restore success, queue recovery, and unresolved data-quality findings.

### Evidence standards

Every success claim should identify the baseline, period, sample, data source, owner, calculation method, and limitation. A dashboard screenshot without this context is not sufficient evidence for a policy decision.

## 14. Responsible product and marketing language

### 14.1 Approved language

Use phrases such as:

- “UniPortal is designed to connect the university lifecycle.”
- “The current source line supports…”
- “The institution can configure…”
- “The workflow is ready for controlled staging and UAT.”
- “Provider activation is subject to certification.”
- “The intelligence features support human review.”
- “Production activation is subject to institutional and technical evidence.”

### 14.2 Language to avoid

Avoid:

- “Fully autonomous university.”
- “Zero-risk ERP.”
- “Automatically compliant with every regulation.”
- “AI decides who graduates.”
- “All integrations are live.”
- “Every user can see every student.”
- “No data cleanup or training is required.”
- “Production certified” before the release gates are complete.

### 14.3 Why honesty is commercially valuable

Decision-makers are more likely to trust a product team that distinguishes implemented functionality from configuration and certification. An honest limitation creates a clear path to evidence. An exaggerated promise creates a risk that may surface during procurement, audit, security review, or implementation.

## 15. Final executive checklist

Before presenting:

- Confirm the current release and its readiness status.
- Confirm the audience and the decision you want from them.
- Prepare a 15-minute and a 45-minute version.
- Use only approved illustrative or masked data.
- Test the mockup assets and any live environment.
- Prepare the failure-handling script.
- Bring the technical manual for follow-up questions.

During the presentation:

- Begin with institutional pain, not software features.
- Explain that UniPortal connects people, process, evidence, and next action.
- Show different role contexts.
- State when data is illustrative.
- Explain the human governance boundary.
- Ask the audience which journey should be piloted.
- Record policy, data, security, provider, and adoption questions.

After the presentation:

- Send the deck and this manual.
- Capture decisions, owners, dependencies, risks, and dates.
- Agree the pilot charter and acceptance measures.
- Do not promise production timing without evidence.
- Schedule the next discovery or UAT session.

> **Final message:** UniPortal’s value is not that it makes the university less governed. Its value is that it helps the university make governance, responsibility, student progress, academic evidence, financial control, and institutional priorities more visible and more executable.
